Lab

A standing experiment, not a tip sheet. Each month the machine writes down parlays to fixed recipes before the games start, grades them afterwards, and never edits one; it also keeps every leg on the board and how it ended. When the month closes it learns from it, starts a fresh record, and the new month is run under what it learned, so the next one shows whether the lessons were any good.

Do the chances mean what they say?

Every leg on the board that has settled, each counted once. Said is the chance the model gave; landed is how often it hit. When the two agree the model is honest. The error bar counts a game's legs as one, since they are not independent.

How to read this page

Said vs landed. If the model says 60% for a thousand legs, about six hundred should land. Fewer means it is too confident; the page says so only when the gap is well outside its error bar, and says "too few" under 30 legs.

Return. What a flat $1 on every leg (or $10 on every ticket) would have come back as, at DraftKings' price. A bet at fair odds still loses the margin the book keeps (a few cents), so "−5¢" is the model being no better than the price, not a failure. A result is worth a second look only when it is a couple of error bars from there.

The price. The ledger keeps the last price and chance a leg had before its game started. Only legs with a real DraftKings price count for the price test: game lines, the NBA's and MLB's props, and the NFL's main prop lines in the hours before kickoff. An estimated price is never counted as one.

The month. On the first run of a new month the record is archived and the Lab fits what it taught: a correction to each sport's chances (kept only if it beats the raw chances on legs held out by game), the chances above which the model stopped being believed, and the recipes to run more or less. Those lessons are then fixed for the whole next month, and every leg's raw and corrected chance are kept, so the "is it working?" check at the top is a fair test on games the lessons never saw.
